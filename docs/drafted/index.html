One CSV, two themes, zero config

dstory.draft() profiled mix.csv, picked the scenes, mapped the columns onto cookbook recipes and wrote every headline from computed facts. Each number in the prose is a registered claim the vetter checks. The same draft is built in two themes below.

The code

import pandas as pd, dstory

df = pd.read_csv("mix.csv")            # year, source, share_pct
d = dstory.draft(df, title="Gas took coal's place", theme="warm-print",
                 source="Illustrative power-mix shares (synthetic data)")
print(d.explain())
dstory.build(d.story, "mix", scenes=d.scenes, vet=True)

# or, with no Python at all:
#   dstory draft mix.csv --theme warm-print --source "Illustrative power-mix shares (synthetic data)"

Scenes:
  1. scene-trend: line-reveal over year x share_pct -- time series detected; share_pct is a share, compared in percentage points, so it follows the largest source in 2024 (Gas)
  2. scene-panels: small-multiples of share_pct by source over year -- 4 source values; one shared scale
  3. scene-shift: slope-chart of source, 2014 vs 2024 -- category x first/last period (4 source values present in both)
  4. scene-race: bump-chart of source rank by share_pct -- the top source changed (Coal in 2014, Gas in 2024)
  5. scene-mix: waffle of source by share_pct in 2024 -- share_pct sums to ~100 per year
Claims (6), each checked by the vetter:
  c-trend: Gas share rose 16 points between 2014 and 2024
  c-trend-step: The sharpest move came in 2015, when it rose 6 points from 2014.
  c-shift: Coal share fell 24 points between 2014 and 2024, the biggest shift of any source
  c-shift-contrast: Renewables share, by contrast, rose 8 points.
  c-mix-lead: In 2024, Gas holds the largest share: 43%
  c-mix-next: Renewables is next, at 23%.

A messy export, with an animated opener

generation.csv looks like a real spreadsheet export: every number is text with thousands separators ("410,000"). draft() reads the column as numbers and says so (a cell it can't read would be dropped and named). Because generation adds up across sources, the story opens with an animated Vizzu chart: the total as stacked bars, which morph into one line per source as you scroll.

Eight markets, eight sizes: small multiples and range-dots

metros.csv has home sales for 8 metros, from Phoenix (about 90,000 a year) to Fargo (about 3,400). On one shared scale the small markets would be flat lines along the floor, so draft() gives each panel its own scale, labels it, and tells the reader to compare shapes, not heights. Eight categories are too many for slope-chart labels, so the before/after scene is a range-dots chart. This one is built with --vendor: it works with no network at all.

Every chart ships its data

Open any story above and look under a chart: a collapsed Data panel holds an accessible table of the exact rows behind it, and a Download CSV link that works offline. Nobody wrote either: the bundler adds them to every scene with a dataset, and the vetter checks in a browser that each table and CSV match data.json. In slides mode, press t. Turn them off per scene with "a11y": {"table": false}, or for a whole bundle with dstory bundle --no-a11y-extras.
